How to use this script
──────────────────────
• Speak naturally — these are prompts, not lines to read verbatim.
• Stage directions are in [square brackets]. Follow them as you speak.
• Estimated total time: 8–12 minutes for a full walkthrough.
• For a 5-minute version, skip the sections marked (OPTIONAL).

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
OPENING  (≈ 60 seconds)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[Launch the dashboard: streamlit run dashboard/app.py — make sure it is already
open and visible on screen before you begin speaking.]

"I'd like to walk you through the interactive dashboard I built for this project.
The dashboard is written in Streamlit and sits at the top of the system stack —
it's the part of the digital twin that a human operator would actually interact
with day to day.

The page title reads 'Greenhouse Digital Twin Prototype' and the subtitle
captures the scope: crop growth prediction using sensor data and a stacked LSTM
network. Everything visible here is driven live by the underlying Python modules
— there's no hard-coded sample data in the UI itself.

I'll take you through it section by section: the sidebar controls first, then
the live KPI cards at the top, and then each of the three tabs."


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 1 — SIDEBAR CONTROLS  (≈ 90 seconds)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[Point to or click on the sidebar on the left.]

"The sidebar is labelled 'Dataset Controls' and has three interactive elements.

The first is a numeric input called 'Sample size for real data'. Setting this to
zero tells the application to load the full dataset — in our case the Kaggle AGC
greenhouse CSV. Setting it to, say, 500 loads only 500 records. I built this in
deliberately because during development I needed to iterate quickly, and loading
tens of thousands of rows into Plotly charts every time was too slow. For the
final demonstration I set it to zero to use the complete dataset.

The second control is a dropdown — 'Select Prediction Model' — which lets the
examiner choose between the stacked LSTM and the linear regression baseline.
This single switch changes which model's predictions are displayed in the
evaluation tab. It keeps both models present in the same application rather than
requiring two separate scripts.

[Point to the slider.]

The third control is the most interesting one interactively — a timeline slider.
It ranges from step zero, the very first record in the dataset, all the way to
the last record. When I drag this slider, every element on the page updates
simultaneously: the KPI cards change to reflect the sensor readings at that
moment in time, and the procedural plant on Tab Two re-renders at the
corresponding height.

This is the core of what makes it a digital twin dashboard rather than just a
static report — it gives the user the ability to replay the state history of the
greenhouse and inspect any point in time."


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 2 — KPI METRIC CARDS  (≈ 90 seconds)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[Point to the five metric cards at the top of the main page.]

"Directly below the title, across the full width of the page, there are five KPI
cards — one for each of the canonical fields in my DigitalTwinState schema.

Reading left to right: Temperature in degrees Celsius, Relative Humidity as a
percentage, CO₂ concentration in parts per million, PAR light intensity in
micromoles per square metre per second, and finally Plant Height in centimetres.

Each card shows the current value for whichever step is selected on the timeline
slider. The first three cards also show a delta arrow — the deviation from the
agronomic setpoint. For temperature that setpoint is 22 degrees, for humidity
it's 70 percent, and for CO₂ it's 800 parts per million. A green upward arrow
means the reading is above setpoint; a red downward arrow means it is below.

[Drag the timeline slider to demonstrate the cards updating.]

You can see that as I drag the slider the values change in real time. This is the
state synchronisation property of a digital twin — the dashboard reflects the
physical system's condition at each recorded moment.

I chose these five variables specifically because they are the ones the
literature identifies as primary drivers of tomato growth: temperature and CO₂
govern photosynthesis, humidity governs transpiration, light governs
photoassimilate production, and plant height is the integrated outcome variable
we are trying to predict."


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 3 — TAB ONE: SENSOR TELEMETRY  (≈ 2 minutes)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[Click on the first tab: "📊 Digital Twin & Sensor Telemetry"]

"The first tab is titled 'Digital Twin & Sensor Telemetry'. It receives the
dataset records from step zero up to the currently selected slider position, so
it always shows the history up to the moment in time I've chosen.

[Point to the large dual-axis chart.]

The main chart at the top plots temperature and humidity on the same time axis
using a dual y-axis layout. Temperature is on the left axis in orange, humidity
on the right in blue. I chose a dual-axis design rather than two separate charts
because these two variables are inversely correlated in a greenhouse environment
— when temperature rises, humidity typically falls as the air's moisture-holding
capacity increases — and I wanted that inverse relationship to be visually
obvious to the user.

[Point to the two smaller charts below.]

Below the main chart there are two side-by-side panels. The left one shows CO₂
concentration over time in green. You can see the characteristic daily variation:
CO₂ drops during the daytime photosynthesis window as the plants consume it, and
recovers overnight. The right panel shows PAR light intensity in amber, which
follows a bell-curve pattern centred on solar noon — this is the diurnal light
cycle modelled in the sensor simulator.

All four of these charts are Plotly interactive graphs — the examiner can hover
to see exact values, zoom into any time window, or toggle individual traces on
and off by clicking the legend. That interactivity was one of the reasons I chose
Plotly over static Matplotlib for the dashboard layer, while still using
Matplotlib for the exported publication-quality figures in the results folder."


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 4 — TAB TWO: L-SYSTEM PLANT VISUALISATION  (≈ 2 minutes)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[Click on the second tab: "🪴 Procedural L-System Plant Growth"]

"The second tab renders the procedural plant visualisation component. This is one
of the most visually distinctive parts of the system, so I want to explain both
what it shows and why I included it.

[Point to the rendered plant structure.]

What you see here is a two-dimensional plant structure generated using a
Lindenmayer System — an L-System. The plant is not a photograph or a hand-drawn
illustration. It is generated entirely in code by the PlantRenderer class, which
reads the current plant height from the digital twin state and uses that value to
determine the branching depth and stem length of the grammar expansion.

The L-System works by starting with an axiom — a single symbol representing a
stem segment — and repeatedly applying production rules that replace that symbol
with a pattern of stems, branches, and rotation commands. After several
iterations the string encodes the full branching structure, and a turtle-geometry
interpreter converts that string into Plotly line segments on screen.

[Drag the timeline slider to show the plant changing.]

If I move the slider to an earlier step — when the plant was shorter — the
structure simplifies: fewer branches, less height. As I advance to later steps
the plant grows taller and more architecturally complex, reflecting the
accumulated growth computed by the GDD model in plant dot py.

I want to be explicit about its limitations: this is a morphological
representation, not a quantitative biomass model. I'm not claiming the branching
angle of twenty-five degrees maps to a specific cultivar. The value of this
component is that it makes the digital twin's state legible to a human operator
at a glance — a grower looking at this dashboard can immediately see whether the
plant is at a seedling, vegetative, or reproductive stage without reading a
number."


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 5 — TAB THREE: MODEL EVALUATION  (≈ 2.5 minutes)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[Click on the third tab: "📈 Model Predictions & MSc Evaluation"]

"The third tab is the most directly relevant to the research contribution —
it contains the machine learning evaluation results.

[Point to the metrics table.]

At the top is an interactive data table loaded from results/metrics_summary.csv,
which is written out by the evaluation pipeline after training. It contains one
row per model and columns for MAE, RMSE, R², training time, and inference time.
The table applies conditional formatting automatically: the cells with the lowest
MAE and RMSE values are highlighted in green, as are the cells with the highest
R² — so the better-performing model is immediately obvious.

The stacked LSTM consistently achieves lower error than the linear regression
baseline on these metrics, which answers Research Question Two directly.
However — and this is something I want to be upfront about — the LSTM's
advantage comes at the cost of training time and interpretability, which is why I
retained the baseline rather than discarding it.

[Point to the actual vs predicted plot.]

Below the table there are three visualisation panels. The first shows actual
versus predicted plant height trajectories for both models overlaid on the same
chart. A perfect model would produce a line sitting exactly on top of the ground
truth. You can see the LSTM tracks the trajectory more closely, particularly
through the steeper growth phases in the middle of the sequence, while the linear
regression model tends to lag or smooth over sudden transitions.

[Point to the residuals plot.]

The second panel shows the residual distributions — the signed errors for each
prediction. What I'm looking for here is whether the errors are centred on zero
with no systematic bias, or whether the model consistently over-predicts or
under-predicts. Random scatter around zero is healthy; a structured pattern
would indicate a systematic flaw in either the model or the data preparation.

[Point to the loss curve.]

The third panel is only relevant to the LSTM: it's the training and validation
loss convergence curve across epochs. This tells me whether the model actually
learned during training or whether the gradient updates stalled. A smoothly
decreasing training loss that stabilises without diverging confirms the
optimisation was stable.

I want to flag that these results were generated on the available dataset, which
has limitations I discuss in the dissertation's limitations chapter. The numbers
should be interpreted as indicative of relative model behaviour rather than
absolute deployment-ready accuracy figures."


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
(OPTIONAL) SECTION 6 — ARCHITECTURE & DESIGN DECISIONS  (≈ 90 seconds)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

"Before I finish the walkthrough I want to highlight two architectural decisions
that aren't immediately visible on screen but are important for understanding
why the dashboard is built the way it is.

First, every piece of data the dashboard displays comes from the same canonical
source — the CSV exported by the pipeline and the results folder written by the
evaluator. There is no separate database for the dashboard. This means the
dashboard is also an integration test: if the pipeline has a bug that corrupts
the data, it manifests as a visual anomaly here rather than as a silent failure.
During development, spotting inconsistencies in the charts was actually how I
caught two preprocessing bugs.

Second, the Streamlit caching decorator — @st.cache_data — is applied to the
data loading function. This means the dataset is only read from disk once per
session, not on every slider interaction. Without caching, dragging the slider
would re-read hundreds of megabytes from disk on each movement, making the
interface unusable. The caching decision was a practical necessity rather than
an optimisation afterthought."

system — the digital twin state engine, the sensor telemetry, the procedural

a single interactive interface that a greenhouse operator or researcher can use

I'm happy to navigate to any specific section, adjust the slider, switch the

  LIKELY VIVA QUESTIONS ON THE DASHBOARD — SUGGESTED RESPONSES
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Q: Why Streamlit rather than a more traditional framework like Flask or Django?
A: "Streamlit was the right tool for this scope — it lets me write the
   analytics and the UI in the same Python file, so there's no context switch
   between the data layer and the presentation layer. Flask would have required
   a REST API, a template engine, and JavaScript, which would have tripled the
   implementation complexity for no additional research value. If this were being
   deployed commercially I would revisit that choice, but for a research
   prototype Streamlit was appropriate."

Q: The dashboard uses real data — how do you know it's not just memorising it?
A: "The model is trained on the first 80 percent of the chronologically ordered
   dataset and evaluated on the remaining 20 percent. The dashboard visualises
   the test-set predictions only — the model has never seen those records during
   training. The chronological split is critical: a random split would allow
   future observations to contaminate the training set, which would give
   artificially optimistic metrics."

Q: What happens if someone loads a very large dataset — does the dashboard scale?
A: "The sample-size control in the sidebar exists precisely for this reason.
   The @st.cache_data decorator ensures data is only loaded once, and Plotly's
   WebGL renderer handles large time series reasonably well. For very large
   deployments I would consider pre-aggregating the telemetry and serving it
   from a proper time-series database rather than loading raw CSVs into memory."

Q: The L-System plant — is it scientifically accurate?
A: "It's morphologically plausible rather than scientifically calibrated. The
   branching angle and iteration depth are not derived from cultivar data — they
   are parameters I chose to produce a visually legible structure. The height
   input is real, from the GDD model, but the branching geometry is illustrative.
   A future improvement would be to calibrate the production rules against
   measured tomato plant architecture data."

Q: Why does the delta on the KPI cards compare against fixed setpoints?
A: "Those setpoints — 22°C for temperature, 70% for humidity, 800 ppm for CO₂ —
   are the agronomically optimal values cited in the literature for tomato
   cultivation. The delta gives the operator an immediate at-a-glance signal of
   whether the greenhouse is within the optimal band without requiring them to
   remember the target values. It's a UX decision rather than a modelling one."

Q: Could you add the RL agent's recommended actions to the dashboard?
A: "Yes — that's a natural next step. The RL agent produces a four-dimensional
   continuous action vector at each timestep. Adding a fifth tab that shows the
   agent's recommended heating, humidification, CO₂ injection, and lighting
   settings alongside the current state would close the decision-support loop.
   That's listed explicitly in the future work section of the dissertation."
